# Limits

## The rule

This card exists to stop the skill applying the method where it does not fit.
Four limits:

- **Answer-first assumes an audience that wants the conclusion now.** Where
  consensus must be built, or the reader is resistant, a more narrative order
  can work better.
- **The fix for a resistant reader is to reorder the introduction, not the
  pyramid.** Lead with the Complication and hold the Answer until after the
  argument line has been made. The pyramid underneath — the grouping, the
  order, the support — is unchanged; only the SCQA sequence at the top moves.
- **MECE turns into an obsession** and eats disproportionate time on small
  problems where the boundary between two categories was never going to
  matter.
- **The real value sits in chapters 7–9** — questioning the order of a
  grouping, questioning the problem-solving process, questioning the
  synthesis — and these are also the hardest chapters. Stopping at "answer
  first" is the common failure; it captures the easiest part of the method
  and misses most of it.

## How to test it

Before choosing the introduction order, ask one question: what is the
reader's posture toward this conclusion — **receptive**, **neutral**, or
**resistant**?

- Receptive or neutral: standard S-C-Q-A or A-S-C-Q, Answer stated early (see
  `scqa.md`).
- Resistant: reorder to C-S-Q-A, hold the Answer until the argument has been
  made. Do not skip the Answer — delay it.

## Failure modes

- **Applying the full apparatus to a two-line message.** A Slack reply or a
  one-paragraph email doesn't need a gate log, a synthesis test, and an
  explicit SCQA breakdown — the overhead exceeds the stakes. Match the mode
  to the artefact.
- **Treating the reordered introduction as licence to bury the conclusion
  entirely.** Delaying the Answer for a resistant reader is not the same as
  omitting it. A document that never states its governing thought has failed
  regardless of audience posture.
