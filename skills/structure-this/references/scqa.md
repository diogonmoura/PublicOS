# SCQA

## The rule

The introduction to any pyramid follows the SCQA pattern — four elements, each
doing a distinct job:

- **Situation** — what is uncontested; what the reader already accepts before
  you say anything new. Not background, not everything you know about the
  topic — only what the reader would agree with unprompted.
- **Complication** — what changed, or went wrong, or is now true that wasn't
  before. This is what disturbs the Situation and creates the need for more.
- **Question** — the question the Complication raises for this reader, stated
  as an actual question.
- **Answer** — the idea at the top of the pyramid; the governing thought the
  rest of the document supports.

The order of the four elements varies with the effect you want:

- **S-C-Q-A** — standard; the reader gets the Answer once the Situation and
  Complication have built the need for it.
- **C-S-Q-A** — leads with what went wrong; use for urgency, when the
  disturbance has to land before anything else.
- **A-S-C-Q** — Answer first of all; use when the reader is already waiting
  for it and doesn't need to be walked into the question.

Four document patterns recur (ch. 4): giving an instruction, requesting
funding, explaining how to do something, choosing among alternatives. Each
pulls the SCQA elements slightly differently, but all four resolve to the
same four slots.

## How to test it

Write S, C, Q, and A as four separate sentences, on paper — not paraphrased
in your head.

1. Read the Question alone. Does it end in a question mark, and is it a
   question this specific reader would actually ask?
2. Read the Answer immediately after the Question. Does it answer that exact
   question — not a related or broader one?
3. Read the Situation alone. Would the reader nod, or object to something in
   it? An objection means it isn't Situation — it's Complication, or it's
   argument that belongs lower in the pyramid.

## Failure modes

Never use the situation or context section as a dumping ground for everything
that does not fit anywhere else. If a fact is not needed to make the reader
feel the question, it does not belong in the introduction (ch. 3).

- **Bloated Situation.** Background and history piled in because it seemed
  relevant, none of it building toward the Complication.
- **Missing Question.** S and C are stated but the Question is never written
  down — the reader has to infer it, and often infers the wrong one.
- **Answer that doesn't answer.** An Answer that responds to a different or
  broader question than the one just raised.
